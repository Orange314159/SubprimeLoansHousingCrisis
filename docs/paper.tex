\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Subprime Loans}

Subprime Loans have been the focus of many papers on the 2008 financial crisis lack a single, universally accepted operational definition \cite{mian2009consequences}. Generally, subprime loans are considered loans given to high risk borrowers. This generally means these loans carry higher interest rates or stricter terms, such as larger down payment requirements or steeper penalties. For the purposes of this paper, we will consider any loan with a rate spread greater than or equal to 3.00\% as subprime. This has some drawbacks in cases where the rate spread may not indicate some other requirements of the contract or the loan or may be measured inaccurately \cite{mayer2008subprime}.  

Some argue that origins of subprime loans are found in adjustable-rate mortgages---mortgages where the interest rate for the borrower may change based on external economic circumstances \cite{duke_predatory_lending}. Adjustable-rate mortgages often lead to a predatory structure due to the increased rates over time. This coincides with subprime loans because both generally have harsher terms and so are taken out by people who have poor credit. The prevalence of subprime loans began in the early 2000s and persisted up to the financial crisis of 2008, after which, lending was significantly decreased. This increase in subprime loans during the early 2000s may have driven a shift toward single-family residential construction relative to multi-unit housing. This is because many people who previously did not have access to taking out a loan for the construction of their own house now were given the opportunity as more loan agencies were offering subprime loans to a much wider set of people.

Subprime loans like these were then traded on the public market in many different forms such as collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) and mortgage-backed securities (MBS). A CDO is a financial device that consists of many mortgages and spreads the risk into "tranches" with differing return rates and risk rates. Generally, the rating scheme for CDOs is considered highly misleading because while the senior tranches might be more secure than the mezzanine or equity, the senior tranche is still backed by loans that may default, leading to loss of profits. MBS are nearly equivalent, however, where CDOs target all types of loans, MBS are strictly housing backed loans. 

\subsection{The 2008 Financial Crisis}

The 2008 financial crisis is often attributed to the over-reliance on subprime loans and over-leveraging of the early 2000s. Because many firms in the early 2000s acquired high-risk subprime assets, when those borrowers eventually defaulted on their loans, the firms did not have the capital to back up their highly leveraged positions and, in some cases, were forced to be fully dissolved \cite{lioudis_lehman_brothers}. The consequences of this crisis have been seen across both the housing and economic sectors. In the housing sector, the 2008 crisis led to a crash in housing prices and the fire sale of a large portion of properties.

\subsection{The Current Housing Crisis}

The current housing market is full of difficulty for new and low-income buyers. Average house prices have been rising relative to average income for the past few years, leading to a high cost burden on those wanting to purchase a house. In some cases, nearly 70\% of a household's income is used directly to pay rent \cite{nlihc_problem}. In addition to this, housing has become so overcrowded that many people are forced to live in cramped conditions \cite{naeh_state_of_homelessness}. Proposed causes for this crisis such as the over-indebtedness of households today and rapid urbanization outpacing housing development appear to clarify this connection, but these claims will not be rigorously evaluated in this study. 

\subsection{Theoretical Impacts of Subprime Loans}

The expansion of subprime loans in the early 2000s plausibly affected long-run housing outcomes such as with housing insecurity in the late 2010s. The first way in which housing insecurity may be impacted is through overcrowding. As greater proportions of housing were zoned for single-family housing rather than multi-family housing and zoning is a very sticky variable, we may continue to see impacts of less accessible housing for the decades to come. This would provide us a positive relationship between subprime percentage and overcrowding in homes. The second factor that may be considered for housing insecurity is cost burden. As subprime loans led to increased interest rates for loans, the long term impacts of these rates may have lead to a perpetuation of high interest rates and difficulty in purchasing housing in certain areas.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Analysis Framework}

In order to preform this analysis we leveraged python and its associated packages. Specifically: NumPy, Polars, and Pandas were used to manipulate and structure the data from the given sources. In order to perform the analysis, Matplotlib, StatsModels, and GeoPandas were used. We used the included OLS methods from StatsModels to perform the bitartrate and multivariate regressions for both the overcrowding and cost burden models. 

\subsection{Data}

We collected loan data for this study from the HMDA. From our collected dataset, we analyzed loan data from the HMDA from 2004 to 2007. Data from 2000 to 2006 was sourced from OPENICPSR \cite{openicpsr_hmda_2000_2006}, and data from 2007 -- 2010 was sourced from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau website \cite{cfpb_hmda_2007_2010}. These data were used to generate the percentage of subprime loans for each census tract over this time period. Specifically, we focused on the rate spread variable to classify loans as subprime or prime. We used the ACS 5-year estimates \cite{census_acs_2016_2020} to obtain the data for our dependent variables and control variables. We used data from the Housing, Socioeconomic, and Demographic datasets for this analysis. All of the ACS data came from the 2016 -- 2020 time frame. In order to maintain normalized and well sectioned areas we constructed a cross-sectional dataset at the census tract level. Some of the models included use spatial data for the shapes of the census tracts, and this data came from IPUMS NHGIS \cite{ipums_nhgis}. While the dependent variable (housing insecurity) is being measured ten years after the measurement of the independent variable, we are explicitly looking at how subprime loans may have very long term impacts on housing. 

\subsection{Dependent Variables}

The dependent variable considered for this study was housing insecurity. While housing insecurity is a complex topic with many factors that comprise it, we used cost burden and overcrowding as proxies for housing insecurity. In this study, we consider someone to be "cost burdened" by their housing if greater than 35\% of their income has gone to housing in the past 12 months. Rather than the cutoff supported by the HUD (30\%), we chose 35\% as our cutoff to account for areas with high cost living and non-fixed housing costs. We consider the overcrowding rate of a census tract to be calculated with the following formula (Housing Units With More than 1 Person per Room) / (Number of Housing Units). Overcrowding will be considered to be a rate from [0-1] whereas cost burden is a scaled percentage between 0 and 100 inclusive. 

\subsection{Lending Variable}

The variable used for lending was rate spread. Rate spread is defined as the loan interest rate minus the average loan interest rate. We classified any value above 3\% as subprime lending and any value below 3\% as prime lending \cite{cfpb_hmda_2007_2010}. This variable is reported to HMDA by lending institutions. When banks give out loans, they store the information about the loan and report the loans by March 1st of the following year. This produces a comprehensive dataset of every loan originated that year, along with location, income, demographic, and other associated data. From here we extracted the loans that exceed 3\% in rate spread and classified those as subprime. We then found the percentage of subprime loans out of all loans taken out in a certain census tract and calculated the percentage of subprime loans for each census tract. This percentage is measured as a rate between 0 and 1 inclusive. 

\subsection{Control Variables}

In order to control for variability in our predicted variables, we included some control variables. We selected to include: White \%, Black \%, Hispanic \%, Undergraduate Graduation \% (of total population), Median Income, Unemployment Rate, and Poverty Rate. All of these variables are taken from the ACS (5yr) report from 2016 to 2020. These data come from surveys and estimates made by ACS over this five-year period \cite{census_acs_2016_2020}. All of these measured percentages are values are normalized between 0 and 1, however median income purely the median income of the given tract measured in thousands of USD. 

\section{Results}

\subsection{Summary Statistics}

We ran our regressions on both dependent variables---overcrowding rate and cost burden. For the overcrowding rate regression, we found that the subprime loan rate was statistically significant in the baseline model and the model including economic variables, but subprime loan rate lost significance after adding in demographic variables. On the other hand, in the cost burden regressions, we found that subprime loan rate was significant across all of our models. Additionally, our best model achieved an $R^2$ of $\approx 0.404$ when predicting overcrowding rate, and $\approx 0.298$ when predicting cost burden.

\section{Discussion}

The loss of predictive power and significance of subprime loan percentage when adding demographic variables to the overcrowding model is revealing. This indicates that a portion of the information encoded by race is shared by subprime percentage, suggesting multicollinearity between subprime rate and the demographic variables. This however, does not include all of the information included by subprime percentage because, as shown in the cost burden models, the subprime percentage remains statistically significant even after including demographic variables. Additionally, we observe that the coefficient on the subprime rate decreases after adding economic variables in the cost burden model. This suggests that the subprime rate also captures a broader systemic economic variable impacting housing security. 

The most notable finding here is the negative correlation between the subprime rate and cost burden. This was contrary to our initial expectation; however, this may be due to omitted control variables. The significant reduction in correlation between subprime rate and cost burden after adding economic variables suggests that including additional economic controls could account for this negative relationship. Specifically, considering the economic status during the loan process such as with the included income data from the HMDA data set could explain some of this negative correlation \cite{cfpb_hmda_2007_2010}. Additionally, our models omit several potentially relevant control categories, such as demographic trends, political factors, and boundary changes in census tracts. 

As mentioned in earlier sections, there is much more to housing insecurity than the cost burden and overcrowding that we were able to consider in this study. Variables such as homelessness, safety, and access to public facilities could all be modeled to get a comprehensive view of the impacts of subprime loans on the current housing crisis \cite{leopold2016improving}. 

\section{Conclusion}

The findings of this study show that while subprime loans may have a significant impact on housing insecurity, further research will be needed to conclusively show the impact. Taken at face value, the study shows that census tracts with a previously greater percentage of subprime loans should expect to have lower cost burdens. However, the study also shows that census tracts with a previously greater percentage of subprime loans should expect to be more overcrowded. Together, these results appear contradictory, indicating that additional confounding variables ought to be considered. 

This suggests that investment stemming from a financial bubble can have both negative and positive impacts on infrastructure. Extending this framework to modern contexts, we might expect results similar to those of a potential AI bubble, drawing parallels to the earlier dot-com bubble.
